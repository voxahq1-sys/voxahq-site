SUSE cuts 190 jobs and names AI as the reason

Home/News/SUSE layoffs

By the VOXA team · memo sent to staff on 2 November 2026

3 November 20265 min readAnnounced 2 November

The short answer

SUSE, the German open-source Linux company owned by Swedish private equity firm EQT, will cut about 10% of its roughly 1,900 staff. That is around 190 jobs. Chief executive Dirk-Peter van Leeuwen told staff in a memo on 2 November that "automation and AI" are changing how software companies operate and that "the requirement for some existing roles has greatly diminished". Affected employees are being told by 4 November.

What the memo actually says

The wording is blunt for a company memo. Van Leeuwen writes that AI capabilities are advancing fast enough to force a rethinking of how the business works, that modern tooling lets teams move with less overhead and flatter reporting lines, and that some roles are simply needed less than before.

What the memo does not include is a number for the productivity gain. There is no line saying a specific team now ships twice as fast, or that a support queue is handled by a model. The link between the AI and the 190 cuts rests on management's account of its own reasoning, not on a published measurement.

The company has not said which roles or which countries are affected. SUSE has offices in several countries, including India, and its engineering work is spread across time zones. That silence matters: the people most likely to be affected find out last.

Why a 190-person cut is worth reading closely

By the standards of this year, 190 jobs is small. It matters because of who is doing the cutting and how openly they say it. SUSE is not a startup trimming to survive a down round, and it is not a giant using a vague "restructuring" line. It is a 34-year-old infrastructure company, the commercial arm behind one of the oldest Linux distributions, telling its own staff that AI changed the staffing maths.

It also lands in the middle of a wider argument. Companies have attributed a growing share of 2026 job cuts to AI, and researchers have pushed back, saying some of those cuts are ordinary cost trimming wearing an AI label. A memo like this one is exactly the kind of document that gets cited on both sides. Supporters point to the CEO naming AI directly. Sceptics point to the missing productivity number.

The timeline the staff are living with

Affected employees are told by 4 November. Employee representatives are nominated the following day, and one-to-one meetings start the week after. That sequence, in Germany especially, comes from consultation rules designed to slow a decision down and give works councils something to argue with. It does not usually reverse the number. It sometimes changes who is included and what the exit looks like.

For anyone outside the company, the useful part is the compressed timeline. Decisions like this usually leak over weeks. Here the whole thing runs from memo to individual conversations in under two weeks.

What to watch next

  • Whether SUSE publishes a headcount split by function, which would show whether engineering, support or back office took the cut.
  • Whether any of the affected work is moved to contractors or to a partner rather than removed outright. That changes the story from "AI replaced it" to "it was outsourced".
  • Whether the next quarter's results show the cost saving the memo implies. A cut of this size should be visible in operating expenses.

For people working inside similar companies, the practical read is not that AI takes jobs in a single sweep. It is that infrastructure firms are now willing to say out loud that it changed their headcount maths, and to act on that within weeks. The teams that survive that logic are the ones whose output can be pointed at directly.
