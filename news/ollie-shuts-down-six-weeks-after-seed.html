Ollie raised $7.5 million in September and shuts down on 6 November

Home/News/Ollie shutdown

By the VOXA team · TechCrunch and the founder's own post, linked below

31 October 20264 min readAnnounced 30 October

The short answer

Ollie, a family AI assistant that ran over text message and promised not to train on your data, told users on Friday that it shuts down on 6 November. It raised a $7.5 million seed round led by Khosla Ventures in September. That is six weeks between the announcement of the funding and the announcement of the closure, and about one week of notice for anyone paying for it.

What Ollie was

San Diego based, founded to do one thing differently: keep the assistant private. It worked through text messaging rather than an app of its own, connected to calendars and email, and handled the unglamorous work of a household: schedules, meals, to-dos, groceries, appointments, payments. For logins it opened a cloud browser session and sent you a link rather than asking for your password.

It was also one of the first consumer AI apps to hold SOC 2, the audit standard that says a company handles data to a documented process. In a market where the privacy claim is usually a paragraph in a pitch deck, that certificate was the product's whole argument.

Why this shutdown is worth a minute of your time

Not because a startup failed. Because of the shape of it. A funded company with a compliance certificate, a subscription product and real users closed six weeks after the round was announced, and users were told with a week to spare. If you build a household routine on an assistant like this, the risk is not that the model gets worse. It is that the company runs out of runway and the thing stops answering.

There is a structural reason the assistant category is hard. The big platforms ship a general assistant for free with the phone, the calendar and the email already inside. A startup has to be better at one narrow job and charge for it, while paying per token for every conversation. Privacy is a real differentiator, but it is a differentiator that costs money to prove and does not reduce the token bill.

The practical lesson for anyone paying for AI tools

  • Keep the exit path. If a tool holds your calendar, your receipts or your family logistics, export what you can while it is alive. A week of notice is normal, not an edge case.
  • Do not build a workflow on a single small vendor. The bigger the dependency, the more the shutdown costs you, and the shutdown is not a reflection of product quality.
  • A compliance certificate is not a runway. SOC 2 tells you how data is handled, not how long the company will exist.

For the broader picture of where the money is going this quarter, our note on OpenAI cutting its own funding target is the other half of the same week: capital is getting more careful, and small subscription products feel it first.

Questions people are asking

When exactly does Ollie shut down?

6 November 2026. The announcement came on 30 October, so users have about a week.

What happens to my data?

Ask for an export now rather than on the last day. The company has not published a data-retention timeline in the announcement, so the safe assumption is that access ends with the service.

Did the funding fail?

The opposite: it raised $7.5 million led by Khosla Ventures in September. The closure suggests the runway and the market did not line up, not that the round did not happen.

Is this a sign the AI assistant category is dead?

No. It is a sign that a general assistant is hard to sell when the phone already has one. Narrow, paid assistants with a clear job are still being funded.

What should I use instead?

Whatever you pick, check two things first: can you export your data, and can you run the same routine without it for a week. If the answer to either is no, the tool is a dependency, not a utility.
