NVIDIA shares fall 7% after report on CEO and healthcare chief

Home/News/NVIDIA report

By the VOXA team · New York Times report of 11 November 2026 and market reaction

11 November 20266 min read

The short answer

NVIDIA shares fell about 7% in after-hours trading on Wednesday after the New York Times reported that chief executive Jensen Huang had a relationship with Kimberly Powell, the company's vice president and general manager of healthcare, who reports directly to him. The report cites five people who worked at the company. NVIDIA declined to comment to the Times, and neither Huang nor Powell has responded publicly. The story lands while NVIDIA's board is already looking into a separate relationship allegation involving Huang.

What was reported

The New York Times published the report on Wednesday, citing five people with knowledge of the company. The central claim is a personal relationship between Huang, 63, and Powell, 48, who runs NVIDIA's worldwide healthcare business and sits in his reporting line.

That is the claim, and it is a claim: NVIDIA declined to issue a statement to the Times, and Powell did not respond to requests for comment from the Times, CNBC or Reuters. Nothing here has been confirmed by any of the parties.

It is the second such allegation in a short window. NVIDIA's board was already investigating a separate relationship involving Huang, which is why the market reaction was immediate rather than gradual.

Who Kimberly Powell is

She is not a peripheral figure. Powell joined NVIDIA in 2008 after an unusual route in: as a college intern at a medical imaging company she called NVIDIA directly to ask whether its graphics processors could be used to analyse medical scans, and the company recruited her about a year later.

She has been vice president of healthcare since October 2017 and has spent more than 18 years at the company. Her remit covers NVIDIA's worldwide healthcare business: the hardware and software platforms for accelerated computing, AI and visualisation that sit under medical imaging, life sciences, drug discovery and healthcare analytics.

In practice that means she is the executive attached to one of NVIDIA's most visible expansion stories, the push from selling chips into selling the compute layer for hospitals, drug discovery and clinical AI. Partners named in NVIDIA's own material include IQVIA for agentic AI in clinical research and Abridge for clinical conversation models.

She also cancelled a scheduled public appearance on the day the reports broke, according to coverage in the Korean press.

The market reaction

The number that matters is roughly 7%: NVIDIA shares fell about that much in after-hours trading once the report circulated. For a company of NVIDIA's size, that is tens of billions of dollars of market value moving on a story that no one involved has confirmed.

That reaction is worth reading carefully. It is not a judgement on the private lives of two executives. It is the market pricing governance risk at a company whose valuation rests on a single chief executive, at a moment when the board is already examining his conduct.

Why a personal story moves a chip company

Three reasons, and none of them are about the relationship itself.

1. Key-person risk is already the bear case. NVIDIA's strategy, its customer relationships and its public narrative are unusually tied to one person. Anything that tests Huang's position tests the thesis, and the market has no way to price "the CEO is under two board reviews" except by selling first and asking later.

2. The reporting line is the governance issue. Powell reports to Huang. A relationship between a chief executive and a direct report is a standard human-resources question at any company, and at a public company it becomes a board question about disclosure, conflicts and process. That is the part a regulator or a shareholder would focus on, not the personal detail.

3. The healthcare business has external partners. When an executive who fronts partnerships with hospitals and life-science companies disappears from a public schedule, partners notice. Enterprise healthcare sales run on continuity and trust, which is why a cancelled appearance is a business signal as much as a personal one.

The bigger picture: NVIDIA's healthcare bet

Strip out the personal story and there is a business fact underneath it that matters more. NVIDIA spent close to two decades turning graphics processors into the default compute layer for medical imaging, then for drug discovery, then for clinical AI. Powell is the executive most associated with that pivot, and the segment is one of the company's clearest hedges against the concentration of its revenue in data-centre GPU sales.

Healthcare is also the segment with the longest sales cycles. Hospital systems buy in years, not quarters, and they buy from people they have met. That is why continuity at the top of a segment is not a soft issue: it is the mechanism by which the pipeline exists at all.

For investors, the question the report raises is not personal. It is whether a governance distraction arrives at the exact moment NVIDIA needs its healthcare story to look stable, with GTC three weeks away and partner announcements already in the calendar.

What is not known

Worth stating plainly, because most of the coverage will not:

  • No confirmation from NVIDIA, Huang or Powell.
  • No statement from the board beyond the existing review of the earlier allegation.
  • No indication of what, if anything, the board's review will conclude, or on what timeline.
  • No company disclosure filed with regulators on the subject at the time of writing.

What to watch next

The board. Whether the existing review is widened to cover this report is the single most consequential detail. A widened review is a governance event; a denial closes it.

The next scheduled appearance. Huang's keynote at NVIDIA GTC in Washington, D.C. on 1 December is the obvious test. A cancellation or a change of format would say more than any statement.

Partner communication. Healthcare partners with active joint announcements are the group most exposed to the noise, and the least likely to say anything publicly.

Questions people ask

Who is Kimberly Powell?

She is NVIDIA's vice president and general manager of healthcare, responsible for the company's worldwide healthcare business. She joined NVIDIA in 2008 and has held the vice president role since October 2017.

Has NVIDIA confirmed the report?

No. NVIDIA declined to comment to the New York Times, and neither Jensen Huang nor Kimberly Powell has responded publicly.

How much did NVIDIA stock fall?

About 7% in after-hours trading following the report.

Is this connected to the earlier board investigation?

The board was already reviewing a separate relationship allegation involving Huang. This report adds to that pressure, but the two have not been formally linked by the company.

Why does the healthcare business matter here?

Powell runs NVIDIA's healthcare segment, which includes partnerships with clinical research and medical AI companies. That makes her a public-facing executive rather than a back-office one.
