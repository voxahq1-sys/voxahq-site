Canva cuts 1,200 jobs and says small changes will not do

Home/News/Canva layoffs

By the VOXA team · Canva announcement of 5 November 2026

6 November 20267 min read

The short answer

Canva is cutting about 22 per cent of its workforce, roughly 1,200 people, either through redundancy or relocation. Chief executive Melanie Perkins told staff that "small changes won't be enough" as the company rebuilds around AI. It is the largest restructuring in the Australian design company's history, and it lands at a moment when the tools Canva sells are being rebuilt by the same technology it is deploying.

What was announced

The cuts were communicated to staff this week. Canva has not published a country-by-country breakdown of who is affected, and it has not said how many of the 1,200 roles are being removed versus moved to another location. The company has framed the change as a structural one rather than a cost exercise: a smaller organisation, organised differently, built around AI-assisted work.

Perkins's language in the internal note was unusually direct for a company that has spent a decade growing headcount. She wrote that she had reached the conclusion that small changes would not be enough. That is a different sentence from the usual "restructuring to focus on our priorities", and it points at a reorganisation rather than a trimming.

The timing is also notable. Canva is private, profitable for years, and has a balance sheet that did not force this decision. It already cut 32 roles in its finance division earlier this year, a round that was never announced publicly. A second, much larger round eight months later suggests the first was a test of a thesis rather than an isolated correction.

The numbers that matter

Twenty-two per cent is the headline, and it is high for a company that is not in financial distress. For context, technology companies that cut in 2026 have mostly done so in single digits, usually framed around a specific division. A cut of this size across the whole company means entire layers of middle management, support functions and specialist teams are being reworked at once.

One thousand two hundred people is also large in absolute terms for Australia, where Canva is one of the few globally competitive software companies. The country's tech sector is small enough that a cut of this size is felt across the local hiring market, not just inside one company.

What is missing from the announcement matters as much as what is in it. There is no published productivity number, no statement about how much faster a team now ships, and no breakdown of which functions are shrinking. As with every AI-attributed cut this year, the claim rests on management's account of its own reasoning rather than a measurement anyone outside can check.

Why design tools are exposed

Design software sits in an uncomfortable place in this cycle. Its users are not engineers who need a compiler, but people producing a specific artefact: a post, a deck, a thumbnail, a product photo. That artefact is exactly what image and layout models have become good at generating. The work does not disappear, but the number of steps between an idea and a finished file drops sharply, and every step removed was somebody's job.

Canva's own product strategy has been to absorb that shift rather than resist it. Magic Studio, its AI feature set, generates layouts, writes copy, removes backgrounds and resizes designs across formats. That is a sensible product decision and an awkward staffing one: the same features reduce the amount of manual production work per design, which reduces how many people you need per design team.

The competitive pressure is coming from two directions at once. Upstarts ship narrow AI design tools that do one job well, and the large platform players bundle similar features into products that customers already pay for. A company selling a horizontal design tool has to fund AI development against both, from the same revenue base.

What it means if you use Canva

For most users, nothing changes this month. The product keeps shipping, the free tier remains, and the pricing you pay today is unlikely to move because of a headcount decision. What does change, slowly, is the roadmap: fewer people on support, fewer on education and templates, and more of the engineering budget pointed at automated generation.

The practical risks for a small business are the ones that always follow a large reorganisation. Support response times get slower before they get faster. Documentation and template libraries go stale. Features that a specific team relied on can be deprecated quietly, because the team that understood why it existed is no longer there.

The sensible hedge is boring: keep your source files in a format you can move, do not build a workflow that only exists inside one vendor's automation, and export your brand assets to a folder you control. None of that is a comment on Canva's future. It is the same advice that applies to every tool you rent.

What to watch

  • Whether Canva publishes a breakdown by function in its next update, which would show whether engineering, support or marketing took the cut.
  • Whether the price of the paid tiers changes. A smaller company with the same AI infrastructure bill usually looks at pricing within two quarters.
  • Whether the free tier narrows. Free tiers are the first thing to be trimmed when a company needs to convert users rather than acquire them.
  • Whether other design platforms follow. If this is a structural read on the category rather than one company's correction, the next announcement will come within weeks.

For anyone working inside a design team, the useful question is not whether AI can do parts of your job. It is which parts of the job are judgement and which are production. The production half is being automated at every company at once. The judgement half is where the remaining roles are being concentrated, and the gap between the two is where this round of cuts landed.
