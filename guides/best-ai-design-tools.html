Best AI design tools for people who are not designers

Home/Guides/AI design tools

By the VOXA team · updated 6 November 2026

6 November 20267 min read

The short answer

Five tools cover almost every design job a non-designer runs into: Canva for general layouts, Photoroom for product photos, Ideogram for images with readable text, Recraft for vector files that stay sharp at any size, and Figma for anything that has to be handed to a developer. Each one is good at one job and mediocre at the rest. The mistake is trying to do all five jobs in one tool.

Pick the job first, then the tool

"AI design tool" is not a category, it is a shelf. The job underneath decides everything: a social post is a layout problem, a product photo is a lighting and masking problem, a logo is a vector problem, and a landing page is a handoff problem. Tools that try to cover all four end up doing each one at 70 per cent, which is fine for a quick post and painful for anything that ships.

So work backwards. Write down the artefact you need, the format it has to be in, and who touches it after you. If the answer involves a printer, a developer, or a brand team, the tool choice narrows immediately.

The shortlist, by job

  • Canva (Magic Studio). The default for anything with a template: social posts, decks, one-page PDFs, simple video. The AI features generate layouts, resize across formats and remove backgrounds. Best when you need something decent in twenty minutes. Weakest at original visual identity, because everything it produces is anchored to the templates everyone else uses.
  • Photoroom. Product photos. Drop the phone shot in, get a clean cutout and a studio background. This is the tool that most replaces a photographer for small ecommerce, and the free tier handles occasional use. Watch the edges on hair, glass and transparent objects.
  • Ideogram. Images that contain words. Most image models still mangle text; this one renders short phrases and signs more reliably than the rest. Good for thumbnails, posters and mock packaging where a word has to be legible.
  • Recraft. Vector output. If the file has to be a logo, an icon set or anything printed large, you need SVG or EPS, not a 1024-pixel JPEG. Recraft generates vector natively, which saves a redraw in Illustrator later.
  • Figma. Not really an AI design tool, and that is the point. It is where a design stops being an image and becomes a file with layers, spacing and states that a developer can build from. Its AI features are conveniences on top of that structure.

Where they actually fail

Three failures show up again and again, and knowing them saves a wasted afternoon.

Text inside images. Any model that draws letters will eventually draw a letter that is almost right. At small sizes it looks fine; printed at A2 it is a typo. If the text matters, set it as text, not as pixels.

Brand consistency. Generated output drifts. Ten posts made in ten sessions will not look like one brand unless you fix colours, type and layout rules in a template and only vary the photo. The template is the product; the generation is the raw material.

Print and file formats. A JPEG that looks sharp on a laptop can be unusable on a 3-metre banner. Anything physical needs vector or a very high-resolution raster, and most AI tools output the latter at best. For embroidery, signage and packaging, plan on a real vector step.

How to judge output in thirty seconds

Zoom to 200 per cent and look at edges. Generated images often have soft, smeared boundaries where objects meet, especially at the corners of shapes. Then look at hands, reflections and shadows, which are where the model's internal physics breaks. Finally, check whether the image makes sense at thumbnail size, because that is how most people will see it.

For layout tools, the test is different: does the design survive a text change? Double the headline and delete a paragraph. If the layout collapses, the template was doing the work and you were not.

What to pay for

Start free and stay free until a specific limit stops you. The usual limits are export resolution, watermark removal, brand kits and seats. Paying for a seat before you have a repeatable workflow is the common mistake: you end up paying monthly for a tool you open twice.

If you are choosing one paid subscription and you make content daily, pick the layout tool, because that is where the time goes. If you sell physical products, pick the photo tool, because a clean product image converts better than a prettier post.

The workflow that actually holds up

Generate raw material, then assemble by hand in a template. In practice: product photo in Photoroom, background or illustration in Ideogram or Recraft, layout and text in Canva, and anything that has to be built handed to Figma. That keeps generation where it is strong and keeps the parts that need consistency under human control.

One rule ties it together: never let the tool own the file format you cannot open elsewhere. Export your brand assets, keep the source files, and assume you will change tools within two years. The design survives; the subscription does not have to.
