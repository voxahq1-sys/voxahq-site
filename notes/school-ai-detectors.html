What school AI detectors actually measure

Home/Notes/AI detectors

By the VOXA team · notes on detection research and school policy

4 November 20264 min readAnalysis

The short version

School AI detectors do not detect AI. They score text against patterns and report a probability, and that score moves when a human edits the text, when a student writes in a second language, or when the essay is short. The research literature has found both false positives and false negatives at rates that should make any disciplinary decision built on a single score unsafe.

What the tool actually does

Every detector on the market is a statistical classifier. It was trained on text labelled as human or machine, and it learned features of that training set: sentence length variance, word frequency, punctuation habits, the predictability of each next word. At run time it compares the essay to those patterns and returns a number.

That number is not evidence of authorship. It is evidence that the text resembles the machine-written examples more than the human-written ones in that particular training set. Change the training set and the same essay scores differently.

Where it breaks

  • Second-language writers. Students writing in English as a second language use simpler, more regular sentence structures. The same regularity appears in machine text, which is why this group has been flagged at higher rates in published testing.
  • Short answers. A 150-word paragraph gives the classifier very little to work with. Short submissions produce the most confident wrong answers.
  • Editing. Run an AI draft through a human rewrite and the score usually drops, without the content changing. The detector is reacting to style, not to provenance.
  • Formulaic writing. Lab reports, legal memos and five-paragraph essays are taught as patterns. Learning the pattern makes your writing look more machine-like.
  • False negatives. A student who prompts a model for a rough, uneven, error-containing draft can score as human. The tools are as weak in that direction, and that direction gets no headlines.

What schools are actually buying

A number and a dashboard. The vendor reports a confidence score, the school treats it as a finding, and the burden lands on the student to prove a negative, which is close to impossible. Several universities have stopped publishing detector results for exactly that reason, and others now require a human review before any accusation.

The honest framing for a policy is: a detector can start a conversation, not end one. It can prompt a teacher to ask about the process, look at drafts, or set an in-class version of the task. It cannot establish that a specific student cheated.

What to do instead

Assess the process, not just the product. Drafts with timestamps, in-class writing, oral defence of a submitted argument, and version history in a document all carry more signal than a probability score. None of them are fooled by a rewrite.

If a school does use a detector anyway, three rules keep it fair: never act on a single score, always show the student the evidence, and always offer a path to demonstrate the work, such as a short oral explanation. Those three rules would prevent most of the cases that end up in the news.

The wider point is that detection is a symptom of assessment design. When the only artefact is a finished essay produced at home, every tool in the chain is guessing. When the process is visible, the guessing stops mattering.
