Most "AI layoffs" are not really about AI

Home/Notes/AI layoffs

By the VOXA team · notes on the SUSE memo and the research behind AI job cuts

3 November 20264 min readAnalysis

The short version

When a company says it is cutting jobs because of AI, that claim is usually taken at face value. The evidence is thinner than it looks. Oxford Economics found that firms often use AI as cover for weak demand or over-hiring, and Gartner expects 30% of people laid off for AI reasons to be rehired by 2029, at a higher cost than keeping them.

Why the claim is hard to check

The AI layoff announcement has become a genre. A memo goes out, one paragraph names automation and AI, and the number is repeated everywhere within the hour. What almost never comes with it is the number that would make the claim testable: how much output per person actually changed, in which function, measured how.

Yesterday's SUSE memo is a clean example. It names AI directly, and it says the requirement for some roles has greatly diminished. It does not say what the new tooling does that the old tooling did not, or how much faster a team now ships. Without that, "AI" is a reason the company is offering, not a cause anyone has demonstrated.

What the research says

  • Cover for other problems. Analysis by Oxford Economics found that a share of AI-attributed cuts line up with weak demand, poor financial performance or earlier over-hiring. AI is the explanation that plays well with investors.
  • Rehiring is common. Gartner projects that 30% of employees laid off because of AI will be rehired by 2029, and that the cost of bringing them back will exceed what it would have cost to keep them.
  • Reversals already happening. Commonwealth Bank of Australia replaced more than 40 customer service staff with a chatbot, found it was not up to the job, and reversed the cuts. Ford reportedly rehired experienced engineers for quality problems the automation could not fix.
  • The labour data is mixed. Goldman Sachs finds that industries with higher AI adoption show weaker job growth, while other surveys find executives expecting AI to increase entry-level hiring. Both can be true at once, which is the point.

How to read the next announcement

Ask three questions. Which roles, specifically? What measured change in output justifies removing them? And is the work being removed, or moved to a contractor, a partner or another country? The answers separate a genuine automation story from a cost cut with a modern label.

If none of the three are answered, the honest position is that the company cut jobs and offered AI as the explanation. That may still be true. It has not been shown.

Why this matters beyond the companies involved

Every AI layoff announcement is used as evidence in a larger argument about whether the technology is destroying work. If a meaningful share of those announcements are ordinary restructuring in disguise, then the argument is being fed by bad data, and the policy response built on it will be aimed at the wrong thing.

That cuts both ways. It does not mean AI is harmless to employment. It means the announcements are a weak instrument for measuring the damage, and the people making decisions should stop treating a press release as a dataset.
