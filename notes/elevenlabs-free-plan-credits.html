The short answer

The free plan gives 10,000 credits a month, about ten minutes of finished speech, three Studio projects, and no commercial licence. Ten minutes runs out faster than people expect because every retake is charged again, and the licence is the line that matters: the moment you publish audio commercially you need Starter at $6 a month, not more credits.

How long ten minutes really lasts

A 1,500-word script is about ten minutes of speech, so a single episode uses the whole month. That is the simple version. The honest version is worse, because of retakes.

A line read wrong, a name pronounced badly, a pause in the wrong place, a sentence that needs a different emphasis - each one is a fresh generation and each one is charged again. In practice, budget twice your finished runtime. A ten-minute video is twenty minutes of credits, and the free plan does not cover it.

The other thing nobody mentions: generation is priced by characters, not by finished minutes. Numbers spelled out, long URLs read aloud and extra punctuation all cost credits without adding a second of usable audio.

The licence is the real limit, not the credits

This is the part that decides whether the free plan is usable for you, and it has nothing to do with how much audio you make.

Free-tier audio cannot be used commercially. That covers client work, monetised YouTube videos, ads, paid courses and anything behind a paywall. If your project is any of those, the free plan is a demo, not a plan, and the answer is Starter at $6 - which gives you the licence plus 30,000 credits, three times what free gives you.

If your project is a personal video, a school project or a test, free is genuinely enough, and the ten-minute ceiling is the only thing you will hit.

What happens when the credits run out

Generation simply stops until the next monthly reset. There is no overage charge and no surprise bill, which is the one thing the free plan does better than a paid plan. The practical consequence is that you find out mid-sentence: a script half-rendered, half silent, and the fix is either waiting or upgrading.

How to make the free credits stretch

  • Write the script once, properly. Every edit after generation costs credits again. Fix the writing before you generate.
  • Generate in small blocks. A paragraph at a time means a bad take costs one paragraph, not the whole episode.
  • Keep sentences short. The model breathes at full stops, and long sentences force a regeneration to get the pacing right.
  • Test the voice on one sentence first. Stability and similarity decide whether you will need five takes or one - the settings that matter are in our ElevenLabs settings guide.
  • Do not generate audio you will throw away. Drafts in a text editor are free; drafts in audio are credits.

When the free plan is genuinely enough

Three cases. A one-off personal project where nobody is paying you. Testing whether a voice fits before committing to a plan. And short social clips where ten minutes a month covers several videos if you script them tightly.

Every other case - client work, monetised content, anything published under a brand - needs the licence, and that starts at $6.

What the credits do not include

Credits pay for generation. They do not pay for the things around it, and that gap is where people get caught.

Dubbing counts. If you dub a video into another language, that is a second generation at full character cost, not a discount on the first. A ten-minute video in three languages is thirty minutes of credits.

Regenerating a single word costs a whole take. If one word is mispronounced, you regenerate the line it sits in. There is no per-word credit pricing.

Storage and history. Free keeps three Studio projects. Once you delete a project to make room, the audio is gone unless you downloaded it, and re-rendering it costs credits again.

The voice library is not a licence. Using a community voice on free does not make the output yours, and the commercial restriction applies to the audio regardless of whose voice it was.

How this compares to paying per word elsewhere

Most voice tools price per character too, but the conversion rate is wildly different. At ElevenLabs' free allowance, 10,000 credits is roughly ten minutes. Some competitors advertise "one hour free" and then charge far more per minute once the trial ends, which is why the free number on a pricing page tells you almost nothing on its own.

The comparison that matters is the price per finished minute after retakes, not the headline credit count. For most people the honest answer is that a $6 plan beats a generous-looking free tier the moment they publish anything.
