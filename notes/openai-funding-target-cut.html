OpenAI cut its own funding target by $10 billion, four days after cutting the valuation

Home/Notes/OpenAI funding cut

By the VOXA team · Financial Times report, with the earlier Bloomberg and CNBC numbers

31 October 20263 min readReported 30 October

The short answer

On 22 October OpenAI lowered the valuation it was asking for from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion and kept seeking up to $30 billion. On 30 October the Financial Times reported it had also cut the amount: $20 billion instead of $30 billion, at the same valuation. Two cuts in nine days, both of them downward, from the company that sets the price for the rest of the market.

The sequence matters more than the number

First the price came down. Bloomberg and CNBC reported on 22 October that OpenAI had moved the valuation it was discussing by $150 billion, from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion before new money, while still asking for up to $30 billion. Then the size came down: $20 billion, per the FT, with the round closing on 30 October and some investors reducing their allocations.

Read as one story, that is a company finding out what the market will actually pay, in real time, at the deadline. It is not a crisis. A $20 billion round at a $1.25 trillion valuation is still the largest private fundraising in the industry's short history, and OpenAI's last round, in March, was $122 billion at $852 billion including the money.

But it is a change in direction. For three years the pattern was that every conversation about OpenAI's valuation ended higher than the last. This month it ended lower, twice.

Why a funding cut reaches you

Prices in this market are set by the companies with the most capital, and the capital just got a little more careful. Three consequences that show up for ordinary users:

  • Cheap tiers get reviewed. A plan sold below cost is a bet on future scale. When the round is smaller than planned, that bet gets a spreadsheet.
  • Long commitments look better to the seller. Annual seats and multi-year enterprise deals are how a company proves predictability to its investors. Expect more of them, and read the renewal clause.
  • Small vendors feel it first. The same week, Ollie announced it was shutting down six weeks after raising a seed round. Capital getting careful is not an abstraction for a company with eight months of runway.

What to watch next

Whether the round closes at $20 billion or comes back up, and what the other labs do with their own numbers. Anthropic's IPO preparation is running in parallel, which gives investors a second way to price this market. If one of them prices below expectations, the whole ladder adjusts. For anyone paying for AI, the number that matters is not the valuation. It is what the plan page says when you renew, and how much advance notice you get before it changes.

Questions people are asking

Is OpenAI in trouble?

No. It cut the size of a record round, from $30 billion to $20 billion, at a $1.25 trillion valuation. That is a repricing, not a rescue.

Why cut the valuation first?

Because investors would not commit at $1.4 trillion. The price came down on 22 October and the amount followed on 30 October, at the deadline for commitments.

Does a smaller round mean worse models?

Not directly. It means less room for loss-making experiments, which usually shows up in pricing and product priorities before it shows up in model quality.

What does this mean for AI prices?

Pressure to make each product pay for itself. Historically that has meant fewer free-tier giveaways and more annual commitments, not an immediate price rise.

Should I lock in a long plan now?

Only if you would keep it anyway. Locking in to protect against a price rise is how people end up paying for a tool they stopped using.
