What an Hour of Your Time Is Worth, Calculated

23 November 2026 · 6 min read · VOXA editorial

Most one person service businesses set an hourly rate by looking at what a competitor charges, then hope the month works out. The number that actually decides whether you keep the business is smaller and easier to work out: how many hours you can bill in a month, and what an hour has to earn to cover everything the business costs before you pay yourself.

The short answer: take the money you want to keep in a year, add the costs of running the business and the tax you will owe, then divide by the hours you can honestly bill. A one person business usually bills 60 to 70 percent of a working month, not 100 percent.

Start with the hours, not the rate

A working month has about 173 hours in it if you count a 40 hour week. A service business cannot sell all of them. Quoting, invoicing, driving, buying materials, answering messages and fixing mistakes are real work and none of it is billable.

Take a realistic month: 40 hours a week, 4.3 weeks, so 172 hours. If 30 percent goes to the unbillable side, you have about 120 hours you can sell. At 50 percent, you have 86. Most solo operators discover they are closer to the second number than the first, and that single discovery changes the rate they need.

Now the money side

Three numbers go on top of your own pay. Overhead, which includes software, insurance, phone, tools, vehicle and any space you pay for. Tax, which for a self employed person in the US includes income tax plus the self employment contribution, so a rough working figure of 25 to 30 percent of profit is safer than pretending it is zero. And the gap between jobs, because a month with a two week hole in the calendar still has the same overhead.

Here is a worked example, kept simple on purpose:

  • You want to take home $5,000 a month before income tax.
  • Overhead runs $1,200 a month, including tools, insurance and the phone.
  • You want to set aside $1,500 a month, which is about 24 percent of the $6,200 that leaves the business before tax.
  • Total the business has to produce: $7,700 a month.
  • Billable hours at 120 a month: $64 an hour. At 86 hours: $90 an hour.

Same person, same take home, and the rate moves by 40 percent depending on how honest the hours estimate is. That is the whole argument for doing the arithmetic. The rate was never a market fact. It was always a function of the hours you can sell.

The three mistakes that make the number wrong

Counting a full month as billable. If the number says 172 hours, the rate comes out low and the month comes up short every single time. The rate looks competitive and the business quietly loses money for a year.

Leaving tax out. A rate that covers overhead and your pay but not the tax bill feels fine for eleven months and then produces a bill you cannot pay. Whatever the local rule, the money is owed the moment it is earned.

Pricing the easy job and not the hard one. A fixed rate assumes every job takes the same time. If half your jobs run long, the average rate has to include that, or the long jobs eat the profit from the short ones.

When to use a fixed price instead

An hourly rate is a floor, not a strategy. Clients buy outcomes. If a job takes you two hours because you have done it three hundred times, charging two hours punishes you for being fast. The fix is to price the outcome using the hourly number as the internal cost of delivery: if the job takes two hours at $90, the floor is $180, and the price you quote is what the result is worth to the client, not what the clock says.

Keep the hourly figure anyway. It tells you when a fixed price is a mistake, and it tells you which clients are actually profitable once the work is done.

The number you should track every month

Two lines are enough. Hours sold, and revenue per hour sold. If revenue per hour falls while hours rise, you are busy and getting poorer, which is the most common way a one person business dies. If hours sold fall while the rate holds, you have a marketing problem, not a pricing problem.

Write both down at the end of each month. After three months you will know more about your own business than any benchmark article can tell you, because the numbers are yours.

What a year of undercharging actually costs

If the gap is $20 an hour and you sell 110 hours a month, that is $2,200 a month, or $26,400 in a year. Nobody decides to give away $26,400. It happens in increments: a quote matched to a competitor instead of to your own numbers, a discount to close a job quickly, an extra revision given away because asking for money felt awkward.

The other cost is harder to see. Underpriced work attracts clients who buy on price, and those clients are the ones who ask for the most. The business ends up with more hours, more messages and less money, and the owner concludes that the market is bad. The market is not the problem in that story.

Where a deposit fits

The rate is only half of cash flow. A business that bills after delivery is lending every client the cost of the job, which for a solo operator can be a month of overhead sitting in someone else's bank account. A deposit, even 30 percent, changes the shape of the month without changing the price. For repeat clients it is a formality. For new clients it is also a filter, because the ones who refuse to pay anything up front are the ones who were never going to be easy.

Three questions worth answering honestly

How many hours did you bill last month? Not worked, billed. If you do not know, start counting tomorrow, because every other number here depends on it.

What does an hour cost you before you earn a cent? Overhead divided by billable hours. Most people have never calculated it and are surprised by how high it is, because a phone, a vehicle and insurance cost the same in a slow month as a busy one.

What would you have to charge to take two weeks off? That single question exposes whether the rate covers a business or only covers a job. A rate that only works when you never stop is not a rate. It is a wage with extra risk.

What to do this week

  1. Open last month and count the hours you actually billed. Not the hours you worked.
  2. Add up what the business spent, including the things you pay once a year divided by twelve.
  3. Divide what is left by the hours. That is what your hour really earned.
  4. Compare that with the number this article just helped you calculate. The gap is the thing to fix.

None of this needs software. It needs one honest month and a calculator, and then the decision about whether to raise the rate or sell more hours stops being a feeling.
