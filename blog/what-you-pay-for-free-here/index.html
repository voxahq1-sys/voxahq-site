What people pay for that you can do here for free

21 November 2026 · 8 min read · VOXA editorial

Most small teams pay for four things they could do themselves in an afternoon: a chat subscription to write the copy, a spreadsheet to work out what the tools cost, a consultant to say why Google is not reading the site, and someone to produce the file AI crawlers look for. Three of those four are now buttons on this site, free, with no account.

The short answer: the paid part of most AI and SEO work is not the thinking, it is the doing. A cost calculator, an llms.txt generator and a 15-point AEO check cover the discovery. What you still pay a person for is the implementation, and the judgement when the answer is not obvious.

What people are actually paying for

Strip the marketing off and the subscriptions usually fall into five buckets.

  • A writer. A $20 a month chat tool to draft the page, the email and the product description.
  • A cost tracker. A spreadsheet, or a consultant, to answer one question: what do these tools cost us a year, by seat.
  • A technical audit. Someone to open the site and list what is broken: titles, meta descriptions, schema, internal links, speed.
  • AI visibility help. A new category priced like consulting: the llms.txt file, crawler access, and pages written so an assistant can quote them.
  • Monitoring. A monthly subscription that watches prices and rankings and emails a change.

None of those are bad purchases. The problem is that four of them are sold as recurring subscriptions when the underlying job is a one-off calculation or a single file.

Free tool one: what your AI stack costs

The AI cost calculator exists because of one arithmetic mistake everybody makes. You add up your tools at the headline price, feel fine, and then a year later the card statement says something else.

Pick the tools your team pays for, set the seats, and it shows the monthly total next to the yearly one. The prices in it are read from the vendor pages, the same numbers that sit on the individual tool pages here.

Why it matters: three tools at $20 a month is $720 a year, and that is before anyone upgrades a tier because the credits ran out in eleven days. On a yearly view the decision is usually obvious. On a monthly view it never is.

Free tool two: the llms.txt file

The llms.txt generator does one job. You give it your site name, your description and the pages that matter, and it returns a plain text file you can copy or download.

That file is not a ranking trick. It is a signpost: here is what this site is, here are the pages worth reading, here is where the useful answers live. Crawlers that respect it spend less time guessing.

What people charge for this is not the file. It is the discovery that the file exists. The file itself takes four minutes.

Free tool three: the AEO readiness check

The AEO readiness check runs fifteen checks across four areas: answer-first content, structured data, crawler access, and the trust signals that make a page quotable. You get a score out of 100 and the fixes ranked by weight.

This is the closest thing to a paid audit, and it is honest about its own limits: it checks the page you give it, not the whole site, and it cannot tell you whether the answer is any good. It can tell you whether the answer is findable, which is where most sites lose before the writing is even judged.

What the free tools do not do

This is the part a page selling you something would leave out.

  • They do not crawl your site. You paste a page, they check that page.
  • They do not write the fixes. They name them and rank them by weight.
  • They do not monitor anything over time. Run them again next month.
  • They do not know your customers, your margins or your competitors.
  • Nothing you type is sent to us. The calculator and the checker run in the browser, which is also why they cannot store a history for you.

Where the free version stops and a person starts

A tool can tell you the title tag is 92 characters. It cannot decide what the title should say, because that needs the search intent, the offer, and the thing you refuse to claim.

A tool can score a page 61 out of 100. It cannot rewrite the page for a buyer who has never heard of you.

So the honest split is: use the free tools to find out what is wrong and how big the gap is. Pay someone only when the answer needs judgement you do not have in-house, or implementation nobody has time for. The SEO and AEO service exists for that second case, and it is priced per job rather than as a subscription.

The audit that costs nothing

If you want the same thinking applied to your own site by a person, the free 48-hour audit takes one URL. You get the faults that cost the most, in plain English, within two days. There is no charge and no pitch until you ask for one.

The reason it is free is not generosity. Nine out of ten sites have the same four problems, and saying so takes twenty minutes. The tenth one becomes a client.

How to use all three in ten minutes

  1. Run the cost calculator with the tools you already pay for. Note the yearly number.
  2. Open one important page, run the AEO check, and read the top three fixes only.
  3. Generate the llms.txt file and put it at the root of the site.
  4. Re-run the check in thirty days. If the score moved, keep going. If it did not, the file was not the problem.

What this changes about your bill

If you were about to buy a $49 a month SEO toolkit to get a technical audit, this replaces the first month and tells you whether the other eleven are worth it. If you were about to pay a consultant to write an llms.txt file, this replaces that line entirely.

What it does not replace is the work. No free tool has ever fixed a page. It only tells you where the leak is.

The one thing people overpay for most

Seats. A small team buys a five-seat plan because the per-seat price looks cheaper, then two of those seats never open the tool. The calculator makes this visible in one line: same tools, three seats instead of five, and the yearly number drops by the price of a laptop every couple of years.

The second overpayment is annual billing on a tool nobody has tested for a month. The 20% you save on the year disappears the first time a tier upgrade eats it in week two. Run the tool for a month, then take the discount.

The third is paying twice for the same answer. A price index, a keyword tool and an AI visibility checker all claim to tell you what to do next. Most of what they return is a list of fixes you could have read on the vendor page, ranked by a score nobody explains.

What the paid version honestly looks like

If you do hire someone, ask for three things and nothing else: the faults in the order they cost you money, the fix for each in one sentence, and the one number that proves it worked.

That is a two-page document, not a dashboard. Anyone quoting you a monthly retainer for a technical cleanup is selling monitoring, which is fine, as long as you know that is what you are buying.

For most small businesses the sequence is simpler than the industry makes it sound. Fix the titles and the schema once. Publish the llms.txt file. Write the answer to your most-asked question at the top of the page, in two sentences, before the story. Then leave it alone and go sell something.

Verdict: Pay for implementation, not for discovery. Discovery is three buttons here, and they are free because the arithmetic is simple and the file takes four minutes. Judge them by what they leave you able to do next: a yearly number you can act on, a ranked list of fixes, and one file that makes your site easier to read. If you get those three and still do nothing, the subscription was never the problem.
