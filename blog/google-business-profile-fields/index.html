Google Business Profile: The Fields That Actually Move Calls

20 November 2026 · 9 min read · VOXA editorial

Most local businesses treat their Google Business Profile like a directory listing they filled in once. It is closer to a second homepage, except Google decides which fields matter, and half of them are the ones owners leave blank. The fields below are the ones that move calls, and the order to fix them in.

The short answer: five fields carry most of the weight. Primary category decides which searches you appear in. Business name decides whether you get suspended. Address or service area decides which map you show up on. Hours decide whether people trust the listing. Photos and review replies decide whether they tap call. Everything else is a tie breaker.

Why the profile outranks your website for local calls

When someone searches "plumber near me" on a phone, they see three map results before any website. That block is built from Business Profiles, not from your site. A business with a complete profile and 12 honest reviews will take the call from a business with a better website and a profile that says "hours unknown".

The good news is that the profile is the cheapest asset you own. There is no ad spend and no card on file. The work is filling fields correctly, once, and keeping them true.

The field that decides what you rank for: primary category

Your primary category is the single strongest signal for which searches you can appear in. Pick the wrong one and you are invisible for the search that pays the bills: a "restaurant" that should be a "caterer", a "contractor" that should be an "electrician".

Google's own guidance is narrower than most advice admits: choose the fewest number of categories it takes to describe your overall core business. That means one primary category, plus only the secondaries that describe work you actually want. Adding eight categories to catch every search is not a shortcut, it dilutes what you are known for.

Test it before you settle. Change the primary category, wait a week, and watch which queries bring calls. It is the only honest way to know.

Business name: the keyword stuffing that gets you suspended

This is the field that gets profiles suspended, and the rule is plain. Your name should reflect your business's real world name, as it appears on your storefront, your website, your stationery and how customers know you. Google's example is a 24-hour coffee shop called Shelly's Coffee, entered as Shelly's Coffee, with the address, hours and category in their own fields.

What does not work: "Shelly's Coffee Best Espresso Downtown Open Late". Including that extra information is exactly the thing the guidelines tell you to put in the other sections. If you have been doing it, fix it before someone reports you, because a suspension removes the listing and the reviews with it.

Address or service area, not both

Google wants your address and service area to be accurate and precise, and it wants one profile per business. For a plumber working out of a van, that means hiding the address and setting a service area. For a cafe, it means a real street address and no service area.

Getting this wrong sends the wrong kind of caller. A service area that covers six counties tells people you travel, and then you spend the first minute of every call explaining that you do not go that far. Set the radius to where you will actually drive at 7am.

Hours, including the special ones

An hour of wrong hours is worse than no listing, because the customer drives over. Set regular hours, then use special hours for holidays and one-off closures. If you take calls after hours, say so in the description rather than leaving the profile closed and hoping.

If your hours change by season, put a reminder in your calendar. Nobody updates this field in July, and that is exactly when the calls come from people standing outside.

Photos: what to upload and what to stop uploading

  • Exterior, shot from the street. The single most useful photo for a first-time visitor, because it answers "is this the right door".
  • Interior, wide. Shows size and lighting before they commit to walking in.
  • The team at work. Real staff, not stock. Faces move people more than a logo.
  • The work itself. Before and after for trades, finished plates for kitchens.

What to stop: logo-only galleries, screenshots of reviews, images with text baked in, and the same photo uploaded eight times. Google's content rules are stricter than the photo itself, and a promotional flyer posted as a photo is a policy problem, not a marketing win.

The Q&A section nobody fills

Anyone can ask a question on your profile, and anyone can answer it, including a stranger who is wrong. The fix is to seed the section yourself with the questions you hear on the phone every week, then answer them in a sentence.

Do this for ten questions: price range, parking, whether you take cards, how fast you can come out, whether you do weekends, service area, warranty, deposit, cancellation, and what the customer should have ready when you arrive. It reads as helpful to a caller, and it removes the same five questions from your day.

Reviews: what you can ask for and what gets removed

You can ask every customer for a review. What you cannot do is pay for one, discount in exchange for one, or filter out the customers you expect to be unhappy. Google's prohibited content policy treats incentivised reviews as fake engagement, and the penalty lands on the profile, not just the review.

Two habits that work: ask on the day the job finishes, in person or by text, with a direct link, and reply to every review, including the bad ones. A reply to a bad review is read by every future customer who scrolls that far, and it is the only place on the internet where you get the last word for free.

Services and products list

This is the field that turns a category into specifics. Add each service as its own line with a short description and a price if you publish prices. It is also the field that feeds the "services" tab on mobile, which is where a lot of first taps happen.

Be concrete. "Plumbing" is a category. "Water heater replacement, drain clearing, leak detection, gas line repair" is a services list, and each line is a phrase somebody types into a phone.

Attributes and accessibility fields

Attributes are the small facts that decide whether someone walks in: wheelchair accessible entrance, veteran led, women led, free parking, outdoor seating. They are free to fill and they filter searches you cannot see happening.

Fill the ones that are true and skip the rest. An attribute you cannot honour costs you more than an empty field, because a customer arrives expecting it.

Bookings, messages and the response time you promise

If you turn on messaging, answer it. An unanswered message thread is a public record that nobody is home. The same goes for booking links: a button that opens a calendar with no availability for three weeks reads as a closed business.

Only enable the channels you can staff. A phone number that rings a human beats a chat widget that nobody reads.

The fields that do nothing

Some fields are optional and stay optional. The description box is read by very few people and indexed weakly, so do not spend an afternoon on it. Business "posts" are not a substitute for anything, and the owner's personal profile link does not move local rankings. Effort spent there is effort not spent on the primary category and the review velocity.

A 45-minute cleanup order

  1. Fix the business name so it matches your signage exactly.
  2. Set one primary category, then delete the secondaries that describe work you do not want.
  3. Decide address or service area, and make the radius honest.
  4. Set regular hours, then add the next three holidays as special hours.
  5. Upload six photos: exterior, interior, three of the work, one of the team.
  6. Seed ten Q&A pairs from your call log.
  7. Turn off any channel you cannot answer within a day.

What to measure in 30 days

The profile has its own numbers: calls, direction requests, website taps and messages. Track four weeks before and four after the cleanup, and compare calls and direction requests rather than views, because views move for reasons that have nothing to do with you.

One caution about the arithmetic. If the profile sends 100 views and 8 calls a month, and one call becomes a $400 job, the profile is worth $3,200 at a 100 percent close rate and $800 at a realistic 25 percent. Those are your numbers to fill in, not ours, and any agency quoting a fixed return is guessing on your behalf.

The suspension risk nobody plans for

Profiles get suspended for three reasons more than any other: a name stuffed with keywords, an address that is a house where no customer can visit, and a service area so wide it looks like a directory entry rather than a business. When that happens the listing disappears from maps, the reviews go with it, and reinstatement takes days to weeks.

The defence is boring. Keep the name as it is painted on the door. Use a real address or a real radius. Do not create a second profile for the same business to catch another suburb. Every one of those shortcuts is a report waiting to happen, and a competitor with a grudge is a click away.

Who should not touch the profile at all

If you run a service business from home and do not want your address public, do not create a profile with an address and hope. Set the service area, hide the address, and make sure the verification route you choose does not require a postcard to a place customers cannot visit. If you operate in several cities, one profile with a wide radius is not the answer; a separate legitimate location, staffed, is the only version Google accepts.

Verdict

The profile is not a set-and-forget listing. It is five fields that decide whether you show up, and three habits that decide whether people tap. Fix the name, the category, the area, the hours and the photos this week. Then ask for reviews on every job and reply to all of them. That is the whole job, and it costs nothing but an hour.

Questions

Questions people ask

What is the most important field on a Google Business Profile?

The primary category. It decides which searches you can appear in at all. Everything else improves your odds inside those searches.

Can I put keywords in my business name?

No. Google asks for the name as it appears on your storefront and stationery, and says to put extra detail like address, service area, hours and category in their own fields. Keyword stuffing is a suspension risk.

Should I use an address or a service area?

One or the other. A shop with customers walking in needs a real address. A trade that drives to customers should hide the address and set a service area that matches how far you will actually travel.

Can I offer a discount for a review?

No. Google's prohibited content policy treats incentivised reviews as fake engagement, and the penalty can hit the whole profile, not just that review. Asking for reviews is fine. Paying for them is not.

How many photos should a profile have?

Six is a working minimum: exterior from the street, a wide interior, three of the finished work, and one of the team. Replace them seasonally rather than uploading the same shot again.
