What a Banned Ad Account Costs, in Money and in Weeks

26 November 2026 · 6 min read · VOXA editorial

A restricted ad account is not one bad day. It is a stretch of days in which the machine you built stops, and the bill arrives in weeks rather than dollars. The useful way to size it is on a single mid sized account: what you stop earning, what you have to rebuild, and what never comes back at all.

The short answer: the cash you do not spend is not the loss. On a $3,000 a month account, three weeks down costs roughly $1,150 of contribution you will not earn, and the rebuild afterwards costs about two more weeks of running below your old numbers. The part that cannot be recovered is the event history behind your pixel, which is the reason your best ad set worked in the first place.

Three costs, only one on a receipt

When an account goes down, the first instinct is to count the spend that did not run. That number feels like the loss and it is not, because unspent money is still in your account. The real costs are three, and only the first is easy to see.

  1. Contribution that stops. Every day of spend that does not run is a day of margin that does not arrive. This is the only cost with a clean number.
  2. The learning reset. Ad sets need conversions to settle. A new or rebuilt account starts with no history, so the platform spends your money learning who converts, and the first weeks run worse than the account you had.
  3. The weeks you spend rebuilding. New ads, new audiences, new pages and a slower review queue. That is your time, and it is the part nobody puts in the spreadsheet.

The arithmetic on a $3,000 a month account

Assume a buyer spending $100 a day. Assume every $1 of spend returns $1.80 in revenue and leaves $0.55 after product cost, payment fees and refunds. Those two assumptions are the whole model, and you can swap in your own numbers in a minute.

Contribution while running: $100 a day of spend times $0.55 is $55 a day, or about $1,650 a month. Now take the account down.

  1. 7 days down: $385 of contribution gone. Most buyers absorb this and call it a bad week.
  2. 14 days down: $770 gone, which is close to half a month of margin.
  3. 21 days down: $1,155 gone, plus the two weeks of rebuilding that follow at less than your old performance.
  4. 30 days down: $1,650 gone, and by then the creative you were scaling has usually aged out.

The rebuild is the part buyers forget to add. If the first two weeks after reinstatement run at half the old contribution, a 21 day outage plus a 14 day rebuild lands near $1,540 of margin lost on an account that spends $36,000 a year. Set that against ten minutes of checking a headline before launch and the argument makes itself, every month.

What never comes back

The money is arithmetic. These are the parts that do not have a formula, and they are usually worse.

  • The event history. Your pixel or conversion API has been learning from every sale and every near miss for months. A new account starts from nothing, and the platform's optimisation is only as good as that history.
  • The account's own record. Reviews are attached to the business, not just the ad. A file with notes in it gets read more carefully than a clean one, and that stays true after you are reinstated.
  • The seasonal window. A ban in the two weeks before your best month is not a two week loss, it is the whole quarter's plan.
  • The creative that was working. The ad you finally got right does not carry its performance into a new account. You rebuild it and hope it performs the same way twice.

How accounts actually go down

Three routes, in the order we see them.

Repeated policy violations

One rejected ad is noise. A stack of them across weeks reads as a business that does not fix its copy, and that is when a person looks at the account rather than the ad.

Payment and identity problems

A declined card, a mismatch between the billing name and the business, or a document request nobody answered. Unromantic, common, and easy to fix before it becomes a restriction.

Unusual activity

Sudden location changes, a login from a new country, or an account that starts spending ten times its normal budget overnight. Security systems treat that as a compromise, and a compromised account gets locked.

The cheap insurance

  1. Check the copy before every campaign, not after a rejection. Most bans begin as a rejection somebody resubmitted three times.
  2. Keep one page compliant instead of five clever ones. A policy page, a refund page and a contact page that match the offer remove the most common account level complaint.
  3. Do not edit a live ad set for cosmetics. Every edit restarts learning. Save the change for the next launch.
  4. Keep the billing and identity details current. A card that expires during a busy week is the cheapest ban to prevent and the most annoying to fix.
  5. Answer document requests the day they arrive. These have a window, and the window is not generous.

What this means for a small advertiser

If you spend under $5,000 a month, the arithmetic above is the whole case for spending ten minutes on copy before a launch. A three week outage is 4% of your year and about 10% of a quarter's margin, and you carry none of the upside that a big advertiser carries when they run the same risk.

None of this is a reason to run a second account to dodge a restriction. That turns a temporary problem into a permanent one. It is a reason to make the first account boring: clean copy, current billing, one compliant page, and no resubmitting the same line until it sticks.

There is a second reason the arithmetic matters at this size. A small account has no margin to absorb a bad month, so the rebuild cost lands on money you were going to put somewhere else: the next test, the next creative, the month you finally hire someone. A restriction does not just stop the machine, it eats the budget that was going to make the machine better.

Questions people ask

How long does a restricted ad account usually stay down?

It varies by reason. A payment problem can be fixed in a day. A policy restriction often takes days to weeks, and a permanent one does not lift at all. Plan for weeks, not hours.

Does starting a new account help?

No. It restarts your event history, resets your learning and puts you back in front of the same reviewers. Fix the cause on the account you have.

Can I keep running ads while a review is open?

Sometimes, at reduced delivery, and nobody tells you the rule in advance. Treat any open review as a reason to slow down, not a reason to launch the biggest campaign of the month.

Is a pre-flight check worth it for a small budget?

At $100 a day, one avoided three week outage pays for years of checking. The check is free to run 50 times a month, so the question is really whether you will remember to do it.
