How to Market a Small Business on $200 a Month: Channel by Channel

19 November 2026 · 7 min read · VOXA editorial

Two hundred dollars a month is enough to find out whether marketing works for your business. It is not enough to run four channels at once, which is what most small businesses try, and why most of them conclude that marketing does not work.

The short answer: pick one paid channel, put the whole $200 into it, give it six weeks, and count the enquiries that come in. Spend nothing on the other three until that one is answering. The free channels run alongside it the whole time.

Before you spend a dollar, do the three free things

Most small businesses skip these because they are boring and slow. They are also the reason a $200 budget either works or disappears.

  • Claim and fill your Google Business Profile. Hours, categories, photos, service area, and a real description. For anything local, this is the first thing a buyer sees and it costs nothing.
  • Ask ten past customers for a review. Not a favour, a message with the link and one sentence on what to write about. Reviews move local ranking more than any ad you can buy at this budget.
  • Build one page that makes an offer. One service, one price range, one button. If you cannot say what the offer is in one line, no ad will fix that.

You can run the three channels US small businesses actually get leads from alongside this, but the point is the same: fix the free layer before paying for traffic.

Google Search ads: what $200 actually buys

Search ads are the easiest channel to budget because the unit is a click, and you decide the most you will pay for one. What you cannot decide is the market price of the click.

The arithmetic is simple. At $1 a click, $200 is 200 clicks. At $2, it is 100. At $4, it is 50. At $8, it is 25. If one in twenty of those clicks becomes an enquiry, the same $200 produces ten enquiries in the first case and roughly one in the last.

Searches are worth more when someone is ready to buy now. That is why "emergency plumber" costs more than "how does a plumber work" and why the cheap clicks are usually the ones that never buy. Before you commit the whole budget, run the keyword planner for your own market and look at the top-of-page bid range. If the cheapest realistic click is $6, $200 buys 33 clicks and a single channel test, so put the money into one tight keyword group, not twenty broad ones.

Meta: cheap attention, expensive customers

Meta sells attention, not intent. At this budget you are buying impressions and hoping the creative carries the offer. The trap is the boosted post button, which optimises for engagement because engagement is what the button is built for. Likes are not enquiries.

If you test Meta with $200, the shape that wastes least money is one campaign, one audience, three creatives and a clear objective that is not "engagement". Give it two weeks, then judge it on enquiries and cost per enquiry, not on reach. If you cannot install a pixel or track enquiries, skip Meta for now and put the money into search, where the click itself tells you the intent.

Email: the channel you already own

A list of a hundred past customers costs nothing to mail while you are small, and it converts better than any cold traffic because these people already paid you once.

The mistake is treating it as a newsletter about your business. One mail, one reason, one link: a slot you have open next week, a price change, a service you now do. Send it on a fixed day so people expect it. At a hundred subscribers the tooling is free. Somewhere around five hundred you will pay $10 to $20 a month, which is still the cheapest channel on this list.

Where $200 disappears without a trace

  • Boosted posts. They buy engagement, not customers, and the button is deliberately one tap away.
  • Broad match keywords. They let Google decide what your money buys. Watch the search terms report weekly and cut what does not belong.
  • Display and "audience expansion" defaults. Both are switched on by default in new campaigns and both spend faster than search.
  • Agency minimums. A retainer plus ad spend means the $200 is really $200 of ads and $300 of management before anything is learned.
  • Four channels at once. $50 each buys noise in four places and a conclusion in none.

How to know a channel is working

One number decides everything: what an enquiry costs you, against what a customer is worth. If an average customer pays you $400 and half of them repeat, one enquiry is worth well over $100 in expectation. At that margin, $20 an enquiry is a bargain and $200 an enquiry is a business that cannot scale.

Write the number down before you spend, not after. If you do not know what a customer is worth, no channel can be judged. Most small businesses that "tried ads and it did not work" never worked this number out, so the campaign had nothing to be measured against.

What to do with the first ten enquiries

The money gets you the enquiry. Speed gets you the customer. A reply in ten minutes converts several times better than one the next morning, and at this budget you cannot afford to lose the leads you already paid for.

  • Reply the same day, in writing, with a price range and a next step.
  • Ask one question: what exactly do you need and by when. Not a form, one question.
  • Log every enquiry with its source. In six weeks that log is the only honest report you will have.

The weekly number that keeps you honest

Every Monday, write three figures: spend last week, enquiries last week, cost per enquiry. Nothing else. If spend rises and enquiries do not, cut the campaign that week. Six weeks of that one line tells you more than any dashboard.

A six-week test that fits $200 a month

Week one is free work: profile, reviews, one offer page. Weeks two to five put $200 into a single search campaign with one tight keyword group and a landing page that matches the ad. Week six is the read: how many clicks, how many enquiries, what one enquiry cost, and whether that number is below what a customer is worth to you.

If the cost per enquiry is under your margin, raise the budget. If it is double, the problem is usually the offer or the page, not the channel, and no amount of extra spend fixes a page that does not convert. Verdict: at $200 a month, one channel and one offer beat four channels and no conclusion. If you are not sure which channel, start with search because the click tells you the intent, and keep the free layer running underneath it either way.
