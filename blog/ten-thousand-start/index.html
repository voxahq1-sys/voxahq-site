$10,000 to Start a Business in the US: Four Options, Real Cost and Realistic Profit

18 November 2026 · 7 min read · VOXA editorial

$10,000 is enough to start a business in the US, and not enough to survive a bad choice. The deciding factor is not the idea, it is how much of the money has to leave before the first dollar comes in.

The short answer: a service business is the only model here that can return the $10,000 inside a few months, because you sell labour before you buy inventory. E-commerce and food are capital-first, margin-later. If the money cannot be replaced, start with a service.

Every option below uses planning estimates, not quotes. Prices differ by state and city, so treat them as structure rather than promises.

1. Local service business (cleaning, pressure washing, junk removal)

Startup cost: a used van or trailer, equipment, insurance, a simple site, licensing. Realistically $6,000 to $9,000, with the vehicle being most of it.

Unit economics: a two-person job priced at $350 for four hours. Supplies and fuel maybe $40. Labour is the owner's time at first, which is why the first months feel profitable and the later ones need a second crew.

Break-even: fixed costs of roughly $900 a month (insurance, phone, software, vehicle) need three jobs a week. Everything above that is the owner's income.

The catch: it is a sales business wearing gloves. The work is easy to deliver and hard to sell consistently, and the weather owns your calendar.

2. Small e-commerce brand

Startup cost: first inventory run, photography, packaging, a store, and the ad budget to learn what converts. $10,000 spreads thin: a realistic split is $5,000 inventory, $1,000 content, $3,000 ads, $1,000 buffer.

Unit economics: selling a $40 product at a 60% gross margin leaves $24 to cover ads, shipping and returns. If cost per acquisition is $30, you lose $6 on every new customer and hope the second order saves you.

Break-even: rarely inside six months. The first $3,000 of ads is tuition, not marketing.

The catch: inventory is money that cannot be spent twice. A wrong size curve or a slow mover locks cash in boxes.

3. Food truck

Startup cost: the truck itself, permits, commissary access, equipment, insurance, and a health inspection. A reliable used truck plus fit-out usually starts above $40,000, so $10,000 buys the permits and a lease deposit, not the truck.

Unit economics: prime cost (food plus labour) should stay under 65% of sales. A $12 plate with $4 of food and $3 of labour leaves $5 before rent, fuel and permits.

Break-even: you need volume, and volume needs a location you do not control.

The catch: the highest chance of the four of turning $10,000 into $0 and a lease.

4. One-person agency (web, SEO, ads)

Startup cost: a laptop, a domain, a payment processor, and time. Under $1,000, which means $9,000 stays as runway.

Unit economics: a $1,500 project delivered in two weeks is roughly $750 a week of revenue with almost no cost of goods. Three clients a month is a real income.

Break-even: the first client, if you can find one. The barrier is credibility, not capital.

The catch: income stops when you stop, and the sales work never ends.

The comparison that actually matters

  • Fastest to first dollar: service business and agency.
  • Most capital at risk: food truck, then e-commerce.
  • Most scalable without more capital: agency, then e-commerce.
  • Best use of $10,000 if it is your only money: a service business with a tight equipment budget, or an agency with almost no spend.

The reserve rule

Whatever the model, three months of fixed costs stays untouched. For a service business that is roughly $2,700; for a small shop it can be $15,000, which is how a $10,000 budget quietly becomes a $30,000 obligation.

Businesses rarely die from a bad month. They die from having no cash in the second one, which forces a discount, which lowers the margin, which makes the third month worse.

The costs nobody budgets for

  • Entity and compliance: state filing fees, an annual report, a business licence, sometimes a local permit. A few hundred dollars, not zero.
  • Insurance: general liability for service work, more for anything with a vehicle or a kitchen.
  • Payment processing: roughly 2.6% to 3.5% of every card payment, taken before you see the money.
  • Software: scheduling, invoicing, bookkeeping. Small each, noticeable together, and easy to over-buy in month one.
  • The first hire: the point where the margin you calculated on your own labour disappears.

Choosing by temperament, not by spreadsheet

The spreadsheet says service business. The person matters just as much.

If you can sell and do not mind physical work: local service. It rewards hustle and reliability, and punishes slow quoting.

If you like systems and can write: agency. It rewards clarity and repeatable delivery, and punishes scope creep.

If you enjoy product and detail: e-commerce. It rewards patience and iteration, and punishes impatience with inventory.

If you love food and long hours: a kitchen. It rewards consistency and controls, and punishes almost everything else.

The first ninety days, model by model

Service: days 1-30 buy equipment and get licensed; 31-60 land the first ten jobs through neighbours, local groups and one paid channel; 61-90 raise prices on the jobs that took longest and drop the services that did not pay.

Agency: days 1-30 build one offer page and one sample; 31-60 send a hundred hand-researched emails; 61-90 deliver two projects and turn both into case notes.

E-commerce: days 1-30 pick one product and order samples; 31-60 photograph, list, and run a small ad test; 61-90 decide whether the unit economics survive real acquisition costs.

Food: days 1-90 are permits, commissary, inspection and one soft location. If that timeline looks long, the truck is not the right first business.

The question that settles it

Ask what happens if the first month produces nothing. A service business with $9,000 still in the bank can try again next month. A food truck with a lease and a permit has already spent it. That asymmetry, not the projected profit, is what should decide where the $10,000 goes.

What $10,000 cannot buy

It cannot buy attention. A new business with no audience needs either a channel that already has one (search, maps, referrals) or paid reach, and paid reach at $10,000 total is a small experiment, not a marketing plan.

It also cannot buy time. Every model above takes longer to reach profit than the spreadsheet suggests, and the gap between month two and month five is where most of the money disappears.

The margin illusion

First-year profit often looks healthy because the owner's labour is not counted as a cost. Put a number on your own hours at market rate, subtract it, and many small businesses are actually breaking even or losing money.

That is not a reason to quit, it is a reason to know. A service business paying itself $25 an hour with $8,000 of equipment is doing better than one paying $40 an hour with $40,000 of debt, even if the second looks bigger.

What to do in the first week

Open a business bank account, set up bookkeeping before the first invoice, and write down the three numbers you will check every Friday: cash in the bank, jobs booked, money owed to you. Ten minutes a week prevents the surprise that kills small businesses.

Then get one paying customer. Not a logo, not a licence, not a website. One person who pays you for the thing you said you would do, because that is the only proof the model exists.

The exit question

Ask early what this business is for. A job that pays your salary, an asset you could sell, or a test of an idea. The answer changes decisions: a job optimises for stability, an asset optimises for systems and recurring revenue, a test optimises for learning fast and stopping early.

Businesses that fail often had no answer to that question, so they optimised for nothing in particular and spent the reserve defending a model nobody had chosen.

What makes the second year easier

Two things: recurring revenue and documented process. A service business that adds a small maintenance retainer smooths the months when new jobs are slow, and a written process means the second hire is not a re-learning of everything you already know.

Neither is exciting in month one. Both are the difference between a business that survives its first quiet winter and one that quietly stops answering the phone.

Verdict: with $10,000, buy a business that sells before it stocks. Service work and agency work convert effort into cash the same week; inventory and kitchens convert cash into risk. Pick the model that matches what you can sell, not what looks best on a video.

Questions people ask

Do I need an LLC to start?

Not on day one, but before you sign a lease or take on a client with real liability. An LLC is a state filing with an annual fee, and the cost varies by state.

How much should stay in reserve?

Three months of fixed costs, untouched. Businesses rarely die from a bad month; they die from no cash in the second one.
