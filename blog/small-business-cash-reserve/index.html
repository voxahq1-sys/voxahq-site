How Much Cash a Small Business Should Keep: The 90-Day Rule, With Real Numbers

20 November 2026 · 9 min read · VOXA editorial

Ask ten owners how much cash a business should keep and nine will say three to six months of expenses. Almost none can say what that means for their own numbers, which is why the rule gets repeated and never used. Here is the version you can actually calculate.

The short answer: your reserve is 90 days of fixed outgo, plus one payment cycle you are owed, plus the tax you have already collected but not paid. For a service business spending $12,000 a month with 30-day payment terms, that is roughly $36,000 in fixed cost, $12,000 in receivables lag, and whatever you owe the tax office. Not a multiple of profit. A sum of three specific things.

What a cash reserve is actually for

A reserve does three jobs, and each one has a different size.

  • Timing. You pay wages on the 1st and get paid on the 40th. The gap has to be funded from somewhere.
  • Shock. A machine dies, a client pays late by six weeks, a quiet January arrives on schedule.
  • Choice. The ability to say no to a bad job, or yes to a discount on stock, without borrowing at card rates.

Most owners only size for the second one, which is why the number feels arbitrary. Size all three and it becomes arithmetic.

The 3 to 6 month rule and why it is not a number

The conventional benchmark is three to six months of operating expenses, and it is a benchmark for a reason: it is an average across wildly different businesses. A restaurant with daily card settlements and a six-week supplier cycle is not the same animal as a consultant invoicing monthly.

Treat the multiple as a sanity check, not a target. If your calculation lands at two months, you have a specific gap to explain. If it lands at seven, you are probably holding cash you could put to work, and the question becomes where.

Start with your floor: fixed monthly outflow

Separate the money that leaves anyway

Open a bank statement from a normal month and separate the money that leaves whether or not you sell anything. Rent, utilities, insurance, software, loan payments, the base wage bill, your own draw if it is steady. That number is your floor.

The SBA frames the underlying habit well: a balance sheet is a snapshot of where the business stands, and a cash flow projection is the forward view. The reserve is the distance between the two when the projection goes wrong.

Add the payment lag

The lag is a second reserve

If you invoice on net 30 and your customers take 40 days, you are funding 40 days of cost before the money lands. That is a second block of cash, and it is not optional, because it happens in a good month too.

Take your average monthly revenue, divide by 30, and multiply by the days between sending an invoice and seeing the money. A $12,000 month at 40 days is $16,000 in transit at any given moment. That money is real. It is just not in your account.

Add the tax you already owe

Sales tax collected is not revenue. Payroll withholding is not yours. If those sit in the operating account, they look like a comfortable cushion right up until the filing date, and then the business is short by exactly the amount it was congratulating itself about.

The habit that fixes this costs nothing: a second account, and a transfer every time you invoice or run payroll. The reserve calculation should start after that number is set aside, not before.

Add one bad month, not three

The rule of thumb says three to six months. In practice most businesses survive on one genuinely bad month every year or two, and the rest of the year is roughly flat.

Size the reserve for the bad month you can name: the month after your busiest season, the month a big client always pays late, the month the insurance premium lands.

If you cannot name a bad month, look at last year's bank statements. There is one in there.

The 90 day calculation, worked through

Take a service business with $12,000 a month in revenue, $9,000 of that leaving as fixed and variable cost, and customers paying on day 45.

  • Fixed floor for 90 days: 3 x $9,000 = $27,000
  • Receivables lag: $12,000 divided by 30, times 45, equals $18,000
  • Tax set aside: depends on your rate, but it is a real number and it is not spending money
  • Total reserve before tax: $45,000

That is the number this business needs, and it is bigger than most owners expect because the lag is the part nobody counts. A business with card payments settling in two days has almost no lag and a much smaller reserve for the same revenue.

What changes the number

  • Card settlement. Two days instead of 45 days removes most of the lag block.
  • Deposits. Taking 30 percent up front on every job cuts the reserve you need for the same revenue.
  • Contractors paid on completion. The floor drops because you are not carrying payroll through a slow month.
  • Inventory. Stock is cash in a different shape. A seasonal buy needs its own line, not the reserve.
  • Recurring revenue. A predictable base makes the bad month smaller and the reserve smaller with it.

Where to keep it

Boring and reachable beats optimised

Keep it boring and reachable. A separate savings account at the same bank, so a transfer takes one day rather than five. Not in stock. Not in crypto. Not in the operating account where it will get spent on a good idea in month three.

Some owners ladder it into short term deposits so a portion earns something without being locked up. That is fine as long as any single lock expires inside your worst payment gap.

What not to do with it

The reserve is not a war chest for expansion, and it is not the down payment on equipment. The moment it gets used for growth it stops being a reserve, and the next slow month becomes a credit card month at an interest rate that eats the margin you were protecting.

The same logic applies to using it to cover an owner's draw above what the business earned. That is not a reserve doing its job. That is a loan from next quarter.

When you can hold less

Three conditions make a smaller reserve defensible: your revenue is contracted rather than one-off, your customers pay on card, and your costs are mostly variable. If all three are true, 45 to 60 days of fixed cost can be enough. If none are true, three months is optimistic.

How to build it without a loan

  1. Set the target number once, using the three blocks above.
  2. Move a fixed percentage of every deposit, the day it lands, before you pay anything else.
  3. Set the transfer to automatic so it does not depend on your mood.
  4. Do not touch it for anything under a named emergency, written down in advance.
  5. Review the target every quarter, because it moves with your costs.

The cost benefit habit that stops the leak

The SBA's own example is worth copying because it is small and unglamorous. A restaurant considering outdoor seating estimates $5,000 in extra annual profit, against a $1,000 a year permit and $2,000 for tables and chairs. Costs come out of benefits, and the decision is arithmetic rather than enthusiasm.

Run that on every recurring expense once a year. Software nobody logs into, a subscription for a service you stopped using, a card fee you could avoid. Each one is a permanent small drain on the same cash you are trying to protect.

The two numbers that make owners feel richer than they are

Bank balance and profit are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where most cash surprises come from. A healthy balance in the last week of a strong quarter includes money that is already promised to suppliers, to the tax office and to next month's wages.

The honest test is simple: subtract everything you already owe, including the tax, and see what is left. If the answer is negative in a good month, the business is being funded by timing rather than by margin, and timing always runs out.

Seasonal businesses: size on the trough, not the average

If a third of your year is slow, averaging twelve months hides exactly the risk you are trying to cover. Take the slowest quarter's fixed cost and multiply by the number of months you need to get through it without a single new job.

A landscape contractor in the northern states and a bookkeeper in January are solving the same problem from opposite ends of the calendar.

The reserve for a seasonal business is often bigger than the annual average suggests, and it is built in the strong months by design, not by luck.

What a reserve does not fix

Cash on hand will not rescue a business that loses money on every job. A reserve buys time to fix the margin, and time is all it buys. If three months of buffer has been spent and the unit economics have not changed, the next decision is not about cash at all.

It also does not replace credit. A working capital line is for timing, the reserve is for shocks, and using one as the other is how businesses end up with a permanent interest cost they cannot name.

Verdict: Three to six months is a headline, not a plan. Do the three blocks: 90 days of fixed cost, the payment lag you are actually living with, and the tax you have already collected. Write the number on one line, move it out of the operating account, and review it every quarter. If you cannot fund it yet, the fix is not a loan, it is deposits up front and a smaller floor to carry.

Questions people ask

How many months of expenses should a small business keep in cash?

Three to six months is the common benchmark, but the number that matters is your own: 90 days of fixed cost, plus the payment lag you actually live with, plus tax you have already collected.

Is the reserve the same as profit?

No. Profit is what the business earns. The reserve is cash set aside so timing gaps, shocks and slow months do not become borrowing.

Should I keep the reserve in the operating account?

No. A separate savings account at the same bank keeps it one day away and stops it being spent on a good idea in month three.

Can I use the reserve to buy equipment?

Then it is not a reserve. Growth spending should come from profit, a loan or an owner contribution, and the buffer stays funded underneath it.

What if my customers pay by card?

You need less. Card settlement in a couple of days removes most of the receivables lag, which is the block most owners forget to count.
