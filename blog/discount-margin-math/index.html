What 12% off actually costs you: discount math on a service business

21 November 2026 · 9 min read · VOXA editorial

Twelve percent sounds like a small number, and it is the number written on most coupon codes, including ours. On a service job it is not small. It comes straight out of profit, not revenue, and profit on a service job is a much thinner number than the price tag suggests.

The short answer: a 12 percent discount on a job with a 40 percent gross margin removes about 30 percent of the profit. To earn the same money you need roughly 43 percent more jobs. At a 30 percent margin you need 67 percent more. At 20 percent you need 150 percent more. A discount only works when it buys something back: a repeat order, an idle slot, or a smaller scope.

The arithmetic, on one real job

Take a service that sells for $59 and costs $35.40 to deliver, mostly your time. Gross profit is $23.60, which is a 40 percent margin.

A 12 percent discount takes $7.08 off the price. Revenue is $51.92 and profit is $16.52. You kept 70 percent of the profit and gave away 12 percent of the price. That gap is the whole lesson: a discount measured against revenue is always smaller than the same discount measured against profit.

To make the same $23.60 you made before, you now need 1.43 jobs instead of one. Forty-three percent more work, for the same money.

What the same discount does at other margins

Margin is the number that decides whether a coupon is cheap or fatal. Same 12 percent, four different businesses:

Gross marginProfit beforeProfit at 12% offExtra jobs needed
50%$50$38+32%
40%$40$28+43%
30%$30$18+67%
20%$20$8+150%

Two rows are worth staring at. At a 20 percent margin, a 20 percent discount is your entire profit, and 25 percent means you pay to do the work. No amount of volume saves a job that loses money on every unit.

The formula, so you can run it yourself

Extra volume needed equals the old margin divided by the new margin, minus one. If your gross margin is 40 percent and the discount is 12 points, the new margin is 28, so 40 divided by 28 is 1.43, which is 43 percent more jobs.

This is the number to check before any promotion. It converts a percentage into a workload, and workloads are what you can actually say no to. Asked to approve a coupon, the question stops being does 12 percent feel small and becomes can I find 43 percent more bookings this month.

When 12 percent is the right call

Discounts are not automatically bad. They are a trade, and four trades are usually worth it.

  • It buys a first order with a known follow-on. If the average customer comes back twice, giving up part of the first margin to start the relationship is a purchase, not a loss. Write down the follow-on rate before you do it, or you are guessing.
  • It fills capacity that would otherwise be idle. A slot you cannot store has a real marginal cost near zero, so a discounted booking on a quiet Tuesday adds profit that would not exist. The same discount on a full Friday destroys profit.
  • It removes a deliverable instead of cutting the price. Same $59, fewer hours: a short written list instead of a call. The customer sees a deal, your margin holds, and nobody trains the market to expect the lower number.
  • It is capped and has a reason. A subscriber code with a limit and an end date reads as an offer. A permanent coupon on the pricing page reads as the real price, and then the list price becomes fiction.

When it is just a refund to someone who would have paid

The expensive version of the same discount is the one with nothing coming back. Four shapes to avoid:

  • A coupon visible to everyone, all year. You are not acquiring customers, you are charging less to people who already decided.
  • Discounts on your best-selling item, which needs no help.
  • Discounts to strangers with no follow-on product, where the first order is the only order.
  • Stacking, where a 12 percent code meets a 10 percent seasonal sale and the total quietly passes your margin.

What the offer actually costs us

Being specific is fair, since the code on this site is 12 percent. On a $59 audit with a 40 percent margin, each redemption costs $7.08 in profit, and we need about 43 percent more bookings from the list to break even on the promotion. The cap and the end date exist for that reason: it is an offer to people who chose to hear from us, not a permanent price.

If the audit were a $500 job, the same 12 percent would be $60 per sale, and the decision would be a much more serious one. Percentage discounts scale with price, which is why the same code is trivial on a coffee and dangerous on a retainer.

Three alternatives that keep the price

If the goal is to close a hesitant buyer rather than to run a sale, these do the job with less damage.

Add instead of subtract. Keep $59 and include something that costs you twenty minutes and would cost them $30 to buy elsewhere. The perceived value goes up, the margin barely moves.

Split the payment. Two payments of $30 reads easier than one of $59 for a buyer with a cash flow problem, and costs you nothing.

Guarantee the outcome. A clear refund condition removes the risk that was blocking the sale, and costs nothing when the work is good.

A month with the coupon, and without it

Numbers on a table are abstract. Here is what the same month looks like for a one person service selling a $59 audit at a 40 percent margin, with a fixed cost of $400 a month for tools and hosting.

Without the coupon: 20 bookings at $59 is $1,180 revenue, $472 gross profit, and $72 left after fixed costs. Tight, but positive.

With 12 percent off: to keep the same $472, that month needs 29 bookings, because 29 times $16.52 is $479. Twenty-nine bookings is nine more conversations, nine more calls, and about nine hours you may not have.

If the coupon brings 25 bookings rather than 29, the month earns $413 instead of $472. You worked 25 percent harder and took home 12 percent less. That is the outcome most promotions actually produce, and it is invisible if you only look at revenue, which went up from $1,180 to $1,298.

This is why the honest scoreboard for a promotion is profit per month and hours worked, not revenue. Revenue is the number that goes up while you get poorer.

A simple way to test it: run the offer for one month, record bookings, hours and profit, then run the next month without it and compare the same three numbers. Two months of that tells you more than any benchmark, and it costs nothing but attention.

Verdict: A discount is a loan against a future order. If the follow-on exists, 12 percent is cheap. If it does not, you gave money to someone who was already going to pay. Before any coupon, run the division: old margin divided by new margin. If the answer is a workload you cannot deliver, the discount is not a promotion, it is a pay cut.

Questions people ask

Is a 12 percent discount a lot?

Against revenue it is small. Against profit it is not: at a 40 percent margin it removes 30 percent of the profit on that job, and it needs 43 percent more volume to break even.

What margin do I need before I can discount?

Above 40 percent gives you room. At 30 percent a 12 point discount costs two thirds of the profit, and anything below 25 percent margin makes most discounts a loss.

Are percentage discounts worse than fixed ones?

Usually, because they scale with price. $10 off is $10 on a $59 job and on a $500 job. A percentage costs five times more on the larger one for the same psychological effect.

Should I discount to win my first clients?

Only with a written reason and a review date. A first client discount that becomes the standing price is a price cut with extra steps.

How do I raise the price back after a promotion?

Set the end date when you announce it, honour it for anyone who asked before it, and let the next offer be a different thing. A price that returns on schedule is trustworthy; one that drifts back quietly is not.

Does the discount help with ads?

It can lift click-through, which is measured in seconds, and lower the average order, which is measured in months. Compare the two before you call it a win.
