What a Pre-Flight Ad Check Catches, and What It Cannot

23 November 2026 · 6 min read · VOXA editorial

A pre-flight check is a program that reads your ad copy, the text inside your creative and the page it points to, then lists the lines that break a written policy. It is useful precisely because most rejections start in the wording, and the wording is the one part you can fix before you spend anything. It is also limited, and knowing the limits is what stops you from trusting a green light too much.

The short answer: a rule check catches claims that are written down badly. It cannot see a before and after photo, a tone that reads as pressure, or the history of the ad account. Treat it as the check you run before an expensive mistake, not as an approval from the platform.

What a check actually reads

Three inputs, in the order that matters. First the ad copy: the primary text, the headline, the description. Second the text baked into the image, which has to be read out of the pixels because the platform reads it too. Third the landing page, because the promise on the page has to match the promise in the ad, and a mismatch is one of the most common rejection reasons that nobody sees coming.

Everything in a rule based check comes down to one operation: compare a line of text against a pattern and decide whether it breaks a written rule. That is it. There is no model, no opinion, no sense of what the ad is trying to say. Which is exactly why it is fast, cheap and repeatable, and exactly why it has blind spots.

The numbers inside our own checker

FabConvert runs 89 rules. Of those, 55 are blocks, meaning the line should not go live as written. 25 are warnings, meaning the line raises the risk of a review or a lower delivery. The last 9 are style notes that cost nothing but weaken an ad: all capitals, stacked punctuation, a headline that runs long, a vague call to action.

Those 89 rules cluster into families. Health and supplement claims are the largest group, because that is where the enforcement is strictest and where most accounts die. Money promises are next: guaranteed income, guaranteed approval, a win rate printed as fact. Then weight and body language, then urgency and scarcity, then the regulatory words that sound official and are not available to a supplement, like approved by a regulator.

What it catches well

  • Made up certainty. Guaranteed results, 100 percent, always works. A guarantee about an outcome in health or money is a written violation, not a judgement call.
  • Curative language. Cure, treat, heal, fix, end. A supplement cannot cure insomnia, and an ad that says so gets pulled. The same idea written as supports restful sleep is a different claim in the eyes of a reviewer.
  • Named conditions. Diabetes, anxiety, arthritis, high cholesterol. Naming a condition turns a wellness product into a treatment, and that is a policy line, not a style choice.
  • Numbers with no source. Lose 20 lbs in two weeks, 3 times more effective, a win rate of 84 percent. A number in an ad is a claim that has to be substantiated.
  • Borrowed authority. Doctor recommended, as a nurse I use, approved by a regulator. Authority plus an outcome is one of the fastest ways to lose an account.
  • Format problems. Six words in capitals, three exclamation marks, a shortened link in the destination. Small things that raise a score you cannot see.

What it catches badly

Implied claims. An ad can imply a cure without using the word. A checker reads words. If the implication lives in the image, or in the gap between the headline and the page, a rule engine will often pass it and a reviewer will not.

Before and after photos. The policy is about the transformation, and a photograph can show one without a single suspicious word. Reading the text out of an image does not tell you that the two photos are the same person or that the timeline is unrealistic.

Tone. Pressure, fear, shame about a body, a story about a relative who died. None of that is a keyword. A person reviewing the ad reads it in three seconds and reacts to the tone. A rule list does not.

Sarcasm and idiom. Kill it with this one, this stuff is criminal, I would sell a kidney for it. A pattern matcher either misses the joke or flags an innocent line, depending on how tight the pattern is.

Targeting and audience. The same words are fine for one audience and wrong for another. Age, health status and personal attributes change how a line reads, and a text checker does not see who it is going to.

What it cannot see at all

Account history. A brand new ad account and an account with two previous violations are not judged the same way, and no external tool knows your standing. Payment history, page quality, the rest of your funnel, and how many other advertisers are running the same claim this week are all outside the text.

The reviewer. Automated checks and human reviewers are making a decision you cannot fully predict. Any tool that promises approval is lying to you, and that is why our own policy page says plainly that a check does not guarantee an ad will be approved.

The flag is the small half. The rewrite is the work

A list of problems is not a fix. Most buyers already know the line looks risky. What takes the time is writing the version that keeps the offer and survives the policy, then checking that version again to see whether it created a new problem two lines down.

That is why the button in our checker rewrites the flagged lines and re-runs the rules on the result, instead of only printing a red mark. A first rewrite usually moves a red ad to one or two remaining warnings. The second pass is where buyers stop, and it is usually worth the two minutes.

How to use it without over-trusting it

  1. Check before you write the creative, not after. Copy that passes and then gets an image built around it is cheaper than an image built around copy that has to change.
  2. Fix the blocks, read the warnings. A block is a no. A warning is a decision you make with your eyes open.
  3. Recheck after the rewrite. Every change is new text, and new text can create a new flag.
  4. When the platform rejects something the checker passed, add that line as a rule. That is the only honest way a static tool gets better: your own rejections teach it, one line at a time.

Where this leaves you

A pre-flight check is worth its price if it catches one line that would have cost you an account, and it is worth nothing if you treat a green result as permission. Use it to remove the written mistakes, which are most of them, and keep your own judgement for the parts a rule engine cannot read: the photo, the tone, and the promise that only a person can hear.
