What 89 Ad Policy Rules Actually Catch, and the 11 That Slip

26 November 2026 · 7 min read · VOXA editorial

Every ad account has the same bad morning: a rejection notice with a reason three words long. The line that caused it took ten seconds to write. A written rule list is the cheapest way to catch that line before a reviewer does, and it is worth knowing exactly where such a list stops working.

The short answer: a rule list catches the claims that are written down badly. The one we run holds 89 rules, split into 55 blocks, 25 warnings and 9 style notes, covering health, finance, telehealth and platform limits. The eleven gaps below are the ones no rule list can close, and every one of them can still get an ad rejected.

What a rule actually is

Strip the language down and a rule is one comparison: a pattern, a piece of text, a decision. The word cure next to a health claim becomes a block. Guaranteed next to a money amount becomes a block. Number one with nothing behind it becomes a warning. There is no model making a judgement and no opinion about what you meant.

That narrowness is the point. A rule answers the same way every time, it runs on every line you write, and it costs nothing per check. It is also why a rule list can never be a permission slip.

The checklist, in the order the rules run

Every line we check is tested across four families. This is the shape of the list, not a full rule-by-rule dump, because the value is in knowing which family a problem belongs to.

1. Health and body claims

Cure, treat, heal, reverse, clinically proven, doctor recommended, lose a stated number of pounds in a stated number of weeks, or anything aimed at a named condition. This family produces the most blocks, because health copy is where ad accounts die. A product can claim how it works. It cannot promise an outcome for a medical condition.

2. Money and income claims

Guaranteed returns, risk free, get rich, make a specific amount per week from home, passive income with no work, and any earnings figure with no evidence behind it. The pattern that trips most often is a number plus a promise in the same sentence: $10,000 a month, and proven, ten words apart.

3. Telehealth and regulated offers

Prescription language, dosing, no consultation needed, eligibility claims tied to age or a condition, and anything that reads like medical advice from a page that is not a clinic. Regulated verticals get their own rules because a rejection there comes from a policy team rather than a filter.

4. Platform limits

Personal attributes such as asking whether the reader has a condition, before and after framing inside the copy, all caps, fake countdowns, and text length where a platform sets a written limit. These change most often, because each platform edits its own policy pages and the rules have to follow.

The eleven that slip through

Here is the honest list. Each of these has caused a real rejection that no text check could have prevented, and each one is your job rather than the tool's.

  1. The photo. A before and after body shot, a visible scar, a person who looks unwell. The words pass and the image is what the reviewer actually saw.
  2. The voiceover. A claim spoken over a demo is still a claim. If the caption is clean and the narration says it works in three days, the rule list read the wrong file.
  3. Whether the claim is true. A rule checks shape, not proof. Clinically proven without a study behind it is a problem no pattern match can see.
  4. Tone. Shaming, pressure and fear with no banned word anywhere. Four sentences that read like a threat still get rejected by a person.
  5. Your account history. The same line from a clean account and from one with three rejections this month does not get the same answer.
  6. What the page does after review. A reviewer sees one version of your lander and a visitor sees another. That gap is a ban reason, and it lives in your tracking setup, not your copy.
  7. Who you target. Neutral copy aimed at people with a health condition stops being neutral in context. The rules read words, not your audience settings.
  8. The date. The same offer is fine in April and a problem during a news cycle about the category.
  9. The country. Copy that is fine for a US audience can be blocked in the UK or Germany. One ruleset cannot hold every regulator at once.
  10. The promise behind the price. A $9 offer that cannot be delivered for $9 is overselling, and no pattern in the text knows your fulfilment.
  11. The reviewer. A person makes the final call. Any tool that promises approval is guessing, and that includes ours.

Where the blocks do the work

Fifty five of the 89 rules are blocks, and that ratio is deliberate. A warning is a line you can defend in a review queue. A block is a line you should not write at all, because it fails the same way every time and there is no version of it that survives. When we look at the rejections users paste in, the interesting part is rarely the block. It is the warning that a buyer chose to keep, on a page where three other warnings were already live. One warning is a decision. Four in a row is a pattern that a reviewer reads as carelessness, and carelessness is what gets the whole page looked at.

How to run this checklist in ten minutes

  1. Write the copy before the creative. Checking a draft is cheaper than rebuilding an image around copy that has to change.
  2. Fix blocks, read warnings. A block is a no. A warning is a decision you make with your eyes open.
  3. Read the reason, not just the flag. The reason tells you which family you landed in, and the family tells you what to change.
  4. Recheck the rewrite. New wording creates new patterns. Most rewrites move a blocked ad to one or two warnings, and that second pass is where buyers stop too early.
  5. Add your own rejections as rules. The line that slipped past you is the only rule worth adding that week.

What the list is worth

A checklist like this pays for itself when it catches one line that would have cost an account, and it costs you nothing when it catches nothing. Treat a clean result as a shorter list of mistakes, not as approval. The photo, the voiceover, the account history and the reviewer stay yours.

Questions people ask

How many rules does a proper ad policy check need?

Enough to cover the four families above and no more. We run 89 because health and finance copy needs depth. A store selling furniture does not need the same list, which is why a checker should let you switch rule groups off.

Does a passing check mean the ad will be approved?

No. It means the written copy did not trip a known rule. Approval is a platform decision made by a filter and a person, and neither one reports to us.

How often do the rules change?

Platform limits change most, usually when a policy page is edited. Health and finance patterns move slowly because they rest on regulation rather than fashion. Our own list gets a line added every time a user posts a rejection we did not catch.

Is a checklist enough without a rewrite?

A flag is half the work. The line still has to be rewritten so that it keeps the offer and passes the rules, and that rewrite is what most buyers underestimate.
